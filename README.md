## About

Original student work is often the best way to demonstrate the potential of a platform in teaching. This example emerges from a second-year undergraduate digital-humanities course at the University of British Columbia, Okanagan, called “Digital Detection,” taught by this volume’s co-editor, Emily Christina Murphy from 2018 – 2020. Students pitched and iteratively developed a final project in Twine. They could create an interactive essay, a story, or a game. The assignment required that no matter what students produced in Twine that they must engage with course content and that their assignment must advance an argument based on evidence. For essays, stories, and games alike, this last requirement drew from the thinking of digital humanists like Stan Ruecker and Alan Galey and new media theorists like Lev Manovich, each of whom assert that digital or electronic making is interpretive and argumentative. Galey and Ruecker, in particular, exhort scholars and, to a lesser extent, teachers to “take seriously the suggestion that a digital object can embody an argument.” 

<br/>Student Jordan Pike created the story The Grand Goulding Hotel, setting up a science-fiction, murder-mystery scenario. Pike writes in his project proposal, “Imagine a world in which technology allows detectives and criminal investigators to access the minds and memories of witnesses and suspects. How much would this help unravel mysteries? Would different viewpoints and perceptions illuminate or obscure the truth?”

 ###  Opening these files

Download all files to a local folder on your computer or mobile device. Files for the Twine should be kept in the same folder with the same organization. The Twine can be launched by opening "GrandGouldingHotel.html" in a browser. View the storyboard layer by uploading the same file to Twinery.org or a version of the Twine platform on your own computer.
